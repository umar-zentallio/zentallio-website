Zen Rules, Zen Models, Ask Iris — the three layers of Iris, explained

On this page
L1 — Zen Rules: the layer that never needs explaining L2 — Zen Models: the layer that gets better with data L3 — Ask Iris: the layer that speaks your language Why the split matters

7 min read · Product

Every operator we talk to has the same instinct when they hear "AI Decision Platform": they picture one big model that reads everything and tells you what to do. That's not how Iris works, and that's on purpose.

Iris is built from three layers that do different jobs. Understanding the split matters, because it's the difference between an AI system you can trust with a live restaurant floor and one you have to babysit.

L1 — Zen Rules: the layer that never needs explaining

At the bottom is a set of deterministic rules. If COGS crosses a threshold on a given shift, if a cold-chain temperature reading falls outside spec, if a delivery time exceeds its SLA — Zen Rules catches it immediately, every time, with zero ambiguity about why.

Rules don't "learn" and they don't drift. That's the point. For anything safety-critical or compliance-critical — HACCP checks, price-floor breaches, portion tolerances — you want a layer that behaves exactly the same way on day one and day one thousand. Zen Rules is the layer your team never has to argue with, because there's nothing to interpret. It either fired or it didn't, and the condition that fired it is written down in plain language.

This is also the fastest layer. A rule evaluates in milliseconds against live data, which is why it's the first line of defense for anything time-sensitive on the floor.

L2 — Zen Models: the layer that gets better with data

Above the rules sit Iris's forecasting models — gradient-boosted trees (LightGBM and GBT under the hood) trained on your own operational history. This is where Iris moves from "did something cross a line" to "what's likely to happen next."

Zen Models forecast things like expected order volume by hour, COGS trajectory under current demand, labour needs by shift, and delivery load by route. Unlike a rule, a model doesn't need a hard threshold to be useful — it can tell you that Thursday's dinner rush is tracking 12% above forecast before the rush actually happens, giving a manager time to act instead of just a report to read afterward.

Models are retrained on your data, not a generic industry benchmark, which is why the same Zen Model architecture gives different, specific answers for a 12-unit bakery chain and a 140-unit QSR group.

L3 — Ask Iris: the layer that speaks your language

The top layer is where most people actually meet Iris day to day. Ask Iris takes a plain-language question — "what was Android order volume last hour," "how does Austria's COGS compare to plan," "give me this week's revenue by market" — and turns it into a governed query against your real data, safely.

"Safely" is doing real work in that sentence. Ask Iris runs inside read-only scopes, every query is logged, and it never has write access to your systems. It's built so that a shift lead or a CFO can ask a direct question and get a direct answer, without waiting on a report or learning SQL, and without opening a door that shouldn't be open.

Why the split matters

The reason Iris is organized this way — rules, then models, then language — is that each layer earns trust differently. You trust Zen Rules because it's transparent and unchanging. You trust Zen Models because they're tested against your own outcomes over time. You trust Ask Iris because it's constrained to only ever tell you the truth about your own data, nothing more.

Put together, the three layers mean Iris can catch a portion-drift issue on the line (L1), forecast that it'll cost you two points of margin by month-end if unaddressed (L2), and let your COO ask "how bad is it, exactly" in plain English and get a real answer in seconds (L3). That's the whole idea: not one model trying to do everything, but three layers, each doing the one thing it's actually good at.
