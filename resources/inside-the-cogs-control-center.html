COGS rarely blows up all at once. It creeps.

A scoop that's slightly generous. A topping applied a little heavier than spec. A supplier substitution made once during a shortage that quietly became the new normal. None of it looks like a problem on any single ticket — it only becomes visible weeks later, when the monthly P&L lands and someone asks why margin is down two points and nobody can say exactly when it started.

That gap — between when a cost problem begins and when someone notices — is the most expensive gap in F&B operations. The COGS Control Center is built to close it, and this walkthrough shows how, screen by screen.

The walkthrough

One workbench, network to final call

The tour moves from the widest view down to a single decision — each screen narrowing the question until there's something specific to do.

Start with every branch on one screen; end with a specific item, at a specific location, and the action to take on it.

The network overview ranks locations by where cost is running hot. Cost heads and item groups break that down until the variance points at real SKUs. Alerts surface what has already crossed a threshold, forecast shows where the trend is heading if nothing changes, and the final call is where a manager decides — with the evidence already assembled.

The intelligence

Where the intelligence actually lives

The workbench looks like dashboards. Underneath, three layers do the work — deterministic where it must be, predictive where it pays, and conversational where a human needs an answer, not another report.

Layer 1 · deterministic
Zen Rules

COGS threshold monitoring lives here, not in a monthly forecast. A rule watches actual cost per item against its expected spec, shift by shift, and flags the moment a location or item crosses the line — not the month after.

Layer 2 · predictive
Zen Models

Forecast and learn: where cost is heading if the current trend holds, which items are drifting together, and which movements are real signal versus normal day-to-day variance — so alerts are worth acting on, not noise.

Layer 3 · conversational
Ask Iris

Turns "why is margin down?" into an answer. "Which items moved COGS this week, and where?" comes back with the SKUs, the branches, and the likely cause — instead of forty items to audit by hand.

By the time a number is wrong enough to show up in a monthly report, it's usually been wrong for three to six weeks — and every one of those weeks compounds the loss. Which is why the watching happens shift by shift, not in arrears
The final call

From alert to action

Smaller than a price hike

The instinct when COGS drifts is a price increase across the board. Most of the time that's the wrong tool. If the cause is portion drift on three items, the fix is a portion re-spec on those three items — a station guide, a scale check, a quick retrain — paired with a targeted price adjustment only where it's warranted. The Control Center points at the real cause, so the fix is almost always smaller and less disruptive than operators expect.

COGS drift isn't usually a pricing problem or a supplier problem. It's a visibility problem — the gap between when something starts and when someone's looking closely enough to see it. Close that gap, and the number stops being a monthly surprise.
