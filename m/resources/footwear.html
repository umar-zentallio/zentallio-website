Nexus · Supply Chain

Footwear supply is not measured in units, it is measured in runs. An article is only sellable while the middle of its size curve is intact, which makes replenishment a completeness problem rather than a volume one.

Reorder on the hole, not on the total.

An article can be well stocked in aggregate and unsellable in practice, and only a size-level view can tell the difference.

Iris · L1 · Zen Rules 02 / 03

Single-pair transfer engine

In most categories moving one unit between stores is not worth the handling. In footwear it is the whole game — one pair is what turns a dead article back into a sellable run.

01

Transfers are proposed at pair level, because the smallest movable unit is also the one that completes a run.

02

A pair is only moved out of a door where that size is genuinely surplus, so filling one hole never opens another.

03

Handling cost is weighed against the full-price sale the completed run unlocks, which is what makes a single-pair move defensible.

04

Moves are batched by route rather than by article, so twenty-three doors are served as a round rather than as twenty-three decisions.

05

Every accepted and rejected proposal trains the run-integrity model, which is why hole-risk sits at 92.7% rather than at a launch-day number.

Iris · L1 · Zen Rules 03 / 03

Box, shelf & stockroom locator

The system can say the nine is in the building and still lose the sale, because nobody can find it. In footwear the stockroom is not storage — it is the shop floor's other half.

01

Stock is held to a shelf and box position, not just to a door, so “we have it somewhere” stops being an answer.

02

The walk is what costs the sale. A customer waits about ninety seconds before the fitting turns into a browse, and the locator is built around that fact.

03

Positions are corrected as a by-product of picking, so the map stays true without a separate maintenance task nobody has time for.

04

A box that cannot be found is functionally out of stock, and the nightly count treats it that way rather than flattering the run.

05

Accurate positions are what make the nightly run count trustworthy — a completeness figure is only as good as the shelf beneath it.

Iris · L2 · Zen Models 02 / 03

Fit & return-reason learning

A return in footwear is almost always a fit statement, and it is almost always thrown away. Read properly it is the cheapest product research a retailer owns.

01

Return reasons are captured as structured data at the counter, not as free text nobody reads afterwards.

02

Fit behaviour is learned per last and per supplier — a nine that runs small is a property of the mould, not of the customer.

03

What is learned is published back to the wall and to the site, so the next customer is told before they buy rather than after.

04

Persistent fit problems become supplier conversations with evidence attached, which is the only version of that conversation that changes anything.

05

Returns distort run integrity as they come back in, so the two systems read from the same record rather than arguing about stock.

Iris · L2 · Zen Models 03 / 03

Release & resale-pressure demand

Some footwear demand is not retail demand. When resale pressure is on an article, the queue is buying to flip it — and a retailer who cannot tell the two apart buys the wrong quantity and sells to the wrong customer.

01

Resale signal is modelled alongside retail demand, because a release with pressure behind it behaves nothing like a normal launch.

02

Sell-out speed is separated from sell-out depth — an article gone in an hour to forty buyers is a different outcome from one gone in a day to four hundred.

03

Per-customer limits and member rules are informed by the pressure reading rather than applied uniformly to every release.

04

The size curve of a hyped release is not the size curve of a staple, and buying it as if it were is how a run breaks on day one.

05

The model feeds the same article curve as everything else, so a release is planned inside the season rather than beside it.
