Iris · L1 · Zen Rules 02 / 03

Safety & compliance traceability

Children's clothing carries obligations adult clothing does not — cords, small parts, flammability, fibre content. A recall is not a merchandising problem; it is a question of which stores, which batch, and which families, answered within the hour.

01

Batch and supplier are carried on the item record, so a style is traceable back to the run it came from rather than to the season it sold in.

02

Compliance attributes are held as data, not as a certificate in a folder — which is the difference between a rule that can fire and a claim that has to be looked up.

03

A flagged batch is blocked at the till by rule, across all 74 stores at once, rather than by an email asking staff to remember.

04

Affected baskets are identifiable because sizes are recorded per child, so the families who bought it can be reached rather than broadcast to.

05

The audit trail is a by-product of trading, not a document assembled after an incident.

Iris · L1 · Zen Rules 03 / 03

Maternity-to-newborn journey

The most valuable customer a kidswear retailer ever acquires arrives before the child does — and then needs a different department every few weeks for two years. Handled as separate transactions, that customer is acquired and lost repeatedly.

01

Maternity, newborn and infant are routed as one continuous journey rather than three departments that happen to share a customer.

02

An expected date is a schedule, not a note — it tells the system when the next need arrives without anyone asking the customer again.

03

The handover from maternity to newborn is a rule with a date on it, which is the moment most retailers silently lose the family.

04

The first year of sizes feeds the growth-cycle model directly, so this customer's size-up timing is understood earlier than any other's.

05

Repeat-family rate at 47% is measured across the whole journey, not per department, because a department-level number cannot see the leak.

Iris · L2 · Zen Models 02 / 03

Growth-cycle repeat prediction

Every kidswear customer has a built-in reason to come back and a narrow window in which they will. The size-up moment predicted per family, and the offer timed to land before they shop somewhere else.

01

Size-up timing is modelled per child at 88.4% accuracy, from the sizes that family has actually bought — not from a national growth chart.

02

The window matters more than the message. A size-up offer that lands two weeks late lands after the purchase has already happened elsewhere.

03

Size-up capture is measured at 62%, which makes the missed 38% a number a buyer can be held to rather than a feeling about loyalty.

04

The model is the reason the age-band forecast can distinguish a band that shrank from a band that aged — those are the same children, differently filed.

05

Repeat families at 47% is the outcome this model exists to move, and it is reported on the board next to margin.

Iris · L2 · Zen Models 03 / 03

Gifting & occasion spike planning

Eid, birthdays and holidays modelled as the lumpy demand they actually are, not smoothed into an average. A spike planned for is a margin event; a spike averaged away is a stock-out followed by a markdown.

01

Occasion demand is modelled as its own shape, because a week that does five times the trade of the week before is not a trend line.

02

Gifting skews the size mix as well as the volume — a gift is bought a size up, deliberately, and the buy should expect that.

03

Occasion and school season compete for the same working capital, and planning them together is the only way to see that competition before it happens.

04

Capacity — stock, staff and fitting room — is planned against the spike rather than against the month it falls in.

05

The same discipline that makes intake week survivable makes every other fixed-date spike survivable, which is why one model serves both.
